[scene_001 | 9s]
A piece of artificial DNA coils around a cancer gene and turns down the volume. The idea had been waiting for decades.

[scene_002 | 9s]
For more than 20 years, David Rusling, at the University of Portsmouth, in England, has studied oligonucleotides that form a triple helix.

[scene_003 | 9s]
They are called TFOs. They recognize an exact sequence of double-stranded DNA and settle onto it as a third strand.

[scene_004 | 9s]
In the test tube they worked beautifully. But inside cells, the chemistry of natural DNA would not let them bind with enough strength.

[scene_005 | 9s]
The hardest regions were those rich in guanine and cytosine, areas that control when a gene is switched on.

[scene_006 | 9s]
The twist: the team added a synthetic base called Z, which is already sold commercially. With it, the TFOs bound with more strength and precision.

[scene_007 | 9s]
They tested it on MYC, a gene that drives many cancers when it speeds up. Its activity dropped between 50 and 80 percent in lab experiments.

[scene_008 | 9s]
They outperformed unmodified DNA and earlier methods. A great deal of research is still needed before thinking about patients.

[scene_009 | 9s]
For now it is a tool for turning genes off and studying what happens. Which gene would you want to silence on demand? That was Datos y ociosidades.
