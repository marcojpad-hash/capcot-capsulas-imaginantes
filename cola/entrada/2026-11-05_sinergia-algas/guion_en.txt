[scene_001 | 9s]
An algal bloom that covers kilometers of sea collapses all at once. And viruses, on their own, cannot fully explain it.

[scene_002 | 9s]
It is Gephyrocapsa huxleyi, a very abundant coccolithophore. When its bloom collapses, fewer than 30 percent of the cells have an active viral infection.

[scene_003 | 9s]
A team at the Weizmann Institute, in Israel, built a synthetic community with algae, viruses, and bacteria isolated from a real bloom.

[scene_004 | 9s]
The result: bacteria significantly increased the algal death caused by the virus. It was not an attack, but a co-infection.

[scene_005 | 9s]
To measure it they created two indices: a synergy index, which measures the strength of that alliance, and a benefit index, which measures how much the bacterium gains.

[scene_006 | 9s]
The champion was Alteromonas: extracts of infected cells attract it, it accumulates in the wreckage of the collapse, and it increases when the bloom falls.

[scene_007 | 9s]
And it is not an isolated case: the synergy also appeared in three other microalgae that are widespread across the planet.

[scene_008 | 9s]
It matters because of carbon. Photosynthetic organisms in the sea fix about 50 gigatons of carbon a year, and much of that happens in blooms like this.

[scene_009 | 9s]
This alliance decides whether that carbon is recycled at the surface or sinks to the depths. How many invisible alliances move the climate? That was Datos y ociosidades.
