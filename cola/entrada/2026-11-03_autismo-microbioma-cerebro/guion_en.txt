Gut microbiome and the brain in autism

[scene_001 | 9s]
What if the gut of a child with autism sent different signals to the brain? UCLA and USC went looking for that conversation.

[scene_002 | 9s]
Eighty-four children and teenagers, ages 8 to 17, took part. Half had an autism diagnosis; the other half did not.

[scene_003 | 9s]
The team analyzed stool samples, scanned each participant's brain, and measured their behavior.

[scene_004 | 9s]
In the autism group they found fewer metabolites that microbes make from tryptophan, an amino acid that comes with food.

[scene_005 | 9s]
One stood out: kynurenate, from the kynurenine pathway. That pathway is tied to brain processing and to cognitive changes.

[scene_006 | 9s]
Lower levels were associated with changes in the activity of the insula and the cingulate, regions linked to emotions and the body.

[scene_007 | 9s]
And the lower they were, the more severe the symptoms. Careful: this does not prove those metabolites cause the change; it points to a link.

[scene_008 | 9s]
The authors propose studying diets, probiotics, or synbiotics. But many children with autism have sensory sensitivities that limit what they eat.

[scene_009 | 9s]
It was the first comprehensive analysis of brain, gut, and microbiome in autism. How far do microbes shape the way the brain processes the world? That was Datos y ociosidades.
